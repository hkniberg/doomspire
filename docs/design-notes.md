# Design Notes

Design principles for card content, written down so future cards stay consistent. Based on the fate deck expansion discussion (July 2026).

## Fate cards

### What they are

One fate card is drawn per round, face up, **before anyone rolls dice**. That timing is the defining fact: fate cards are never a surprise. They are the round's *weather* — everyone sees the same forecast and plans around it, so they reward adaptation, not luck.

### What fate cards should do

1. **Give each round a texture.** "This is the boat round", "this is the round to stay out of the hills." Rounds stop feeling identical.
2. **Rotate the spotlight across strategies.** Each card makes a different engine shine for one round (expansion, fame, boats, knight count, gold, buildings). Many small "your strategy shines today" cards do more for balance than hate cards aimed at the leading strategy — playtests showed that even three targeted hate cards landing on a committed might player did not stop him.
3. **Host the political layer.** Council votes are the one place the table acts as a body: leader-checks, coalitions, revenge. Vote weight = fame is a deliberate counterweight (pure-might players have no political power).
4. **Maintain the board over the game arc.** The island depletes — adventure tokens run out, monsters get hunted. Cards like Oasis Bloom and Beasts Are Stirring are the game restocking itself.

### What fate cards should NOT do

- **Decide the game.** No fate card should hand out or deny a win by itself. Dragon Sleeping / Dragon Off Hunting are at the acceptable ceiling of impact; keep cards of that magnitude rare.
- **Erase someone's round without counterplay.** A global "you do nothing" effect is feel-bad. Lockdown is fine *because* it is vote-gated — the table chose it; that's politics, not weather. Prefer squeezes over walls (e.g. Lean Times raises the dice tax to 3 rather than doubling it, so paying stays a real option).
- **Need an unnamed decision-maker.** Fate cards are communal. Any choice must route through a vote, "each player chooses for themselves", a game-state condition (richest, most fame), or turn order. Never "someone decides."
- **Persist beyond the round.** "This round only" is a hard rule — lingering effects mean memory bookkeeping at a physical table.
- **Be filler.** If a card changes nobody's plan, it's a wasted round of texture. (Example of a rejected card: "knights max 2 steps per die" — dice are D3, so it only affects rolls of 3 and creates no decision.)
- **Target randomly.** Since fate lands before actions, players can't dodge it. Negative effects should be symmetric, vote-gated, or keyed to a game-state condition.

### Deck composition guidelines

- **Vote density around one in three.** Votes are the deck's best feature but also its slowest and most same-y texture. Most cards should be passive events/minors.
- **All singletons, deck larger than the game.** A game runs 15–20 rounds; with ~37+ singleton cards each game sees roughly half the deck, and a different half each time. That alone provides between-game variety — no counts or reshuffling needed.
- **Restrictions are usually less fun than boosts.** Symmetric global restrictions just make a round feel smaller. Restrictions earn their place when they are boosts in disguise (Tailwind), vote-gated (Lockdown), or create positional puzzles (Storm Season).

## Fate cards vs. adventure event cards

Both decks make "something happen", but they differ on every axis that matters:

| | Fate card | Adventure event card |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who | The whole table, symmetric | One player, mid-turn |
| When | Round start, before planning | Surprise, as the risk side of exploring |
| Agency | Votes / conditions / turn order | The drawer's choice, fight, or windfall |
| Tone | Weather, politics, economy | A personal adventure story |

**Rule of thumb: if the drawer has no special role in the card, it belongs in the fate deck.** Global effects can live in the adventure deck when the drawer still has a distinct role (Thieving Crows: drawer picks the resource; Curse of the Earth: drawer decides whether to pay) or when hidden timing is the drama (Dragon Raid). A global effect that gives the drawer nothing to do (e.g. Dragon Hunger) is fate-deck material.

## General card-writing checklist

Before adding a card, check:

1. Does it change at least one player's plan for the round?
2. Is every decision it requires clearly owned (drawer, vote, condition, or turn order)?
3. Does its effect end when the round ends (fate) or when the drawer's interaction ends (adventure)?
4. Does it scale sanely at both ends of the game (round 2 and round 18)?
5. Which strategy does it spotlight — and is that strategy under-served? (Watch the gold path: playtests show nobody gets near the 12-gold win condition.)
6. Does it use rulebook terminology exactly (e.g. "dice tax", not "food tax")?
